# Getting your first role

*Last reviewed: November 2026*

The hardest job to get in security is the first one. Entry-level postings often ask for experience, and many people apply for months. These are the things that consistently shorten that wait.

## Consider the side door

Many security professionals did not start in security. They started in IT support, system administration, networking, development or audit, and moved across once they knew the systems. These roles are easier to get, they pay you to learn the foundations, and internal moves into security are common. If direct applications are not working, a role one step away is a strong plan, not a consolation.

## Build evidence, not just credentials

Employers hiring juniors are trying to answer one question: will this person learn quickly and work carefully? Show them.

- **A home lab** you can describe in detail. See [Foundations](foundations.md#build-a-home-lab) and [Practice labs and platforms](practice.md).
- **Write-ups** of investigations, machines or projects, on a blog or GitHub.
- **A small tool** that automates something real: a log parser, a detection rule set, a script that checks cloud permissions.
- **Contributions** to open-source security projects, even documentation fixes.
- **Competitions:** capture-the-flag events, both attack and defence.

One well-written project you can talk about for twenty minutes beats ten half-finished ones.

## Your CV

- Lead with what you have done, not what you have studied: "Built a home SOC with Wazuh and wrote 15 detection rules mapped to MITRE ATT&CK" is stronger than "Familiar with SIEM tools".
- Use the job's own words. Automated screening matches keywords; the [NICE Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/applied-cybersecurity/nice/nice-framework-resource-center) work roles are a good source of standard terms.
- List relevant certifications clearly, without letting them crowd out practical work. See [Certifications](certifications.md).
- Keep it to one or two pages. Link to your GitHub or blog.

## Interviews

Expect three kinds of question:

1. **Fundamentals:** what happens when you type a URL into a browser; how TLS works; the difference between authentication and authorisation.
2. **Scenarios:** "You see this alert. What do you do?" They are testing your reasoning more than whether you reach the right answer. Talk through it out loud.
3. **Motivation:** why security, why this path, what you have built. Your projects answer this.

Saying "I don't know, but here is how I would find out" is a good answer. Bluffing is a bad one.

## Communities

Joining a community gets you advice, referrals and early notice of openings.

- Local chapters of [OWASP](https://owasp.org/chapters/), [ISC2](https://www.isc2.org/chapters) and [ISACA](https://www.isaca.org/chapters) hold regular meetings, often free.
- [BSides](https://bsides.org/) conferences are low-cost, community-run and beginner-friendly.
- Online communities on Discord and Reddit are useful for questions. Look for ones with active moderation and a focus on learning.
