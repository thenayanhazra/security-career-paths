# Threat intelligence

*Last reviewed: November 2026*

> Understand who is likely to attack, how they operate and what that means for the decisions an organisation has to make.

Threat intelligence analysts collect information about adversaries, assess it and turn it into something useful: detections for the SOC, priorities for vulnerability management, briefings for executives. The best intelligence answers a question someone actually asked.

## What the work involves

- Tracking threat groups, campaigns and the techniques they use
- Turning reports and indicators into detections and hunting leads
- Assessing which threats are relevant to this organisation, sector and region
- Writing briefings at different levels, from technical notes to board summaries
- Running intelligence requirements: finding out what decision-makers need to know

## Roles along the way

| Level | Typical titles | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Threat intelligence analyst (junior), cyber threat researcher | Collect and process; write technical summaries |
| Mid | Threat intelligence analyst, threat researcher | Assess and attribute; produce strategic assessments |
| Senior | Intelligence lead, principal threat researcher | Set requirements; lead programmes; brief leadership |

**Framework names:** NICE *Threat Analysis*; ECSF *Cyber Threat Intelligence Specialist*.

## Skills that matter

- Analytical rigour: separating evidence from assumption, stating confidence honestly
- Writing that is short, structured and leads with the conclusion
- Attacker techniques and the [MITRE ATT&CK](https://attack.mitre.org/) framework
- Working with intelligence platforms and formats such as STIX
- Languages and regional knowledge are a genuine advantage for some roles

## How people get in

The path draws from unusual places: SOC analysts, journalists, political scientists, military and government intelligence, linguists. A technical foundation is still needed, but structured analysis and writing are what set strong analysts apart.

## Learn for free

- [MITRE ATT&CK CTI training](https://attack.mitre.org/resources/learn-more-about-attack/training/cti/): using ATT&CK for intelligence work
- [MITRE ATT&CK Groups](https://attack.mitre.org/groups/): structured profiles of documented threat actors and the techniques they use
- [Psychology of Intelligence Analysis](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/books-monographs/psychology-of-intelligence-analysis-2/) by Richards Heuer: the classic text on analytical thinking and bias
- [MISP](https://www.misp-project.org/) and [OpenCTI](https://github.com/opencti-platform/opencti): open-source threat intelligence platforms you can run yourself

## Practise

- Pick a threat group, read the public reporting about it and write a two-page assessment of its relevance to a sector of your choice, stating confidence levels and key assumptions
- Map a public intrusion report from [The DFIR Report](https://thedfirreport.com/) to [MITRE ATT&CK](https://attack.mitre.org/) techniques in [ATT&CK Navigator](https://mitre-attack.github.io/attack-navigator/), then write detection ideas for the highest-priority gaps
- Run [MISP](https://www.misp-project.org/) locally, import a public feed of indicators, enrich one event and export a STIX bundle
- Draft a one-page bottom-line-up-front (BLUF) briefing for an executive on a recent campaign, ending with two concrete decisions to take
