# Security leadership

*Last reviewed: November 2026*

> Lead security teams and programmes, and eventually the whole function, by turning technical risk into business decisions.

Leadership is less a separate path than where many paths lead. A team lead still does technical work; a CISO rarely does. The skills change along the way, from expertise to judgment, from doing to deciding, and from talking to engineers to talking to boards.

## What the work involves

- Building and leading teams: hiring, developing, retaining
- Setting strategy and a roadmap tied to business priorities
- Managing budgets and making the case for investment
- Reporting risk to executives and the board in business terms
- Leading during major incidents and crises
- Working with legal, regulators, customers and auditors
- Representing security across the organisation, and sometimes publicly

## Roles along the way

| Level | Typical titles | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| First leadership | Team lead, security manager | Lead a team of specialists; still hands-on |
| Middle | Head of a function (SOC, AppSec, GRC), security director | Lead managers; own a budget; set direction for an area |
| Executive | CISO, chief security officer, VP security | Own security for the organisation; accountable to the CEO and board |
| Advisory | Field CISO, virtual CISO, board adviser | Advise many organisations, often alongside a vendor or consultancy |

**Framework names:** NICE *Executive Cybersecurity Leadership*, *Systems Security Management*, *Program Management*; ECSF *Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)*.

## Skills that matter

- Communicating risk without jargon or fear
- Prioritisation: there is always more to do than budget allows
- People leadership: most of the job is helping others do theirs
- Financial literacy and business understanding
- Composure in a crisis
- Enough technical credibility to be trusted by engineers and to challenge them

## How people get in

From any path, after substantial experience. The jump from expert to manager is the hardest, and not everyone should make it. Many organisations now offer a parallel technical track (staff, principal and distinguished engineer) for those who want seniority without managing people.

## Learn for free

- [NCSC Cyber Security Toolkit for Boards](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/board-toolkit): what boards need from security leaders, from the UK's National Cyber Security Centre
- [NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework): especially its Govern function
- [AI Security Governance](https://github.com/thenayanhazra/ai-security-governance): a risk framework, key risk indicators and board reporting template for AI systems

## Practise

- Volunteer to lead an incident review, a project or a tabletop exercise
- Write a one-page briefing for a non-technical executive on a current threat. Ask one to read it and tell you what they would decide.
- Mentor someone more junior

## Certifications

CISM and CISSP are the most widely requested. CCISO is aimed specifically at executives. See [Certifications](../guides/certifications.md).
