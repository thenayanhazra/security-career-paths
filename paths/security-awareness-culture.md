# Security awareness, culture and human risk management

*Last reviewed: November 2026*

> Help people make safer decisions through role-specific cybersecurity learning, communication and measured improvement.

This work connects security teams with the people and teams they support. It includes finding out what different audiences need, designing useful learning, communicating clearly and checking whether the programme helps people act more safely. It is broader than an annual compliance course or a phishing test. A strong programme supports secure systems and processes; it does not make staff responsible for compensating for poor design.

The work may sit in a security team, learning and development, communications or a mix of them. The balance depends on the organisation: a small company may ask one person to coordinate training, while a large organisation may have specialists for curriculum design, delivery and measurement.

## What the work involves

- Assessing the needs of different roles and teams, then setting clear learning outcomes
- Designing accessible, practical training, guidance, simulations and other learning materials
- Coordinating with security, privacy, HR, communications and business leaders
- Helping teams respond constructively to reports and questions about security
- Measuring learning and programme outcomes, then improving the programme
- Keeping advice current as threats, tools, policy and working practices change

## Roles along the way

| Level | Typical titles | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Security awareness coordinator, cybersecurity trainer, learning designer | Organise sessions, maintain materials and support delivery |
| Mid | Security awareness specialist, security education programme manager | Assess needs, design role-based programmes and evaluate results |
| Senior | Head of security education, security culture director | Set programme direction, secure leadership support and coordinate change across the organisation |

**Framework names:** NICE *Cybersecurity Curriculum Development* and *Cybersecurity Instruction*; ECSF *Cybersecurity Educator*, which also lists *Cybersecurity Awareness Specialist* and *Cybersecurity Trainer* as alternative titles.

## Skills that matter

- Security fundamentals, so guidance is accurate and practical
- Instructional design: turn a real work task into a clear learning objective and useful practice
- Plain-language writing, facilitation and communication across different audiences
- Audience research, accessibility and inclusive learning design
- Evaluation and data literacy, including the limits of each metric
- Empathy and discretion when discussing mistakes, reports or sensitive incidents

## How people get in

People move into this work from learning and development, teaching, communications, customer support, IT or another security role. A useful first step is to build security fundamentals and practise explaining them to a particular audience. Teaching, facilitation, writing and evaluation skills matter alongside security knowledge. Some employers place the role in HR or communications; others expect it to be part of the security team.

## Trade-offs

- **Results take time:** a completed course is easy to count, but lasting changes in decisions and habits are harder to attribute to one programme.
- **The role needs support:** training cannot fix a confusing process, an unsafe default or a product that makes the secure choice difficult.
- **Measurement needs care:** individual tracking can damage trust. Use proportionate, privacy-conscious measures and explain what they can and cannot show.

## Learn for free

- [NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/50/r1/final): a lifecycle approach to building and improving cybersecurity and privacy learning programmes
- [NIST NICE Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/applied-cybersecurity/nice/nice-framework-resource-center): work roles, tasks, knowledge and skills; see *Cybersecurity Curriculum Development* and *Cybersecurity Instruction*
- [ENISA European Cybersecurity Skills Framework](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/topics/skills-and-competences/skills-development/european-cybersecurity-skills-framework-ecsf): role profiles, including *Cybersecurity Educator*
- [CISA Secure Our World](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world): free public cybersecurity awareness materials that can be adapted for learning activities

## Practise

- For a fictional 150-person company, identify three audiences and write one observable learning outcome for each. Explain what work task or risk makes each outcome relevant.
- Create a short, accessible learning activity for one audience using invented data. Include a realistic decision, feedback and a link to the approved reporting route.
- Evaluate a fictional training programme using aggregate scenario responses, questions and learner feedback. State what the results do not prove; do not rank or shame individuals.
- Draft a one-page programme review for a non-technical manager with one improvement to fund and a reasoned way to check its effect.

## Ready to apply

You are ready to interview for an entry-level security education or awareness role when you can:

- Explain how you would identify a specific audience need and turn it into a measurable learning outcome
- Show a clear learning activity or piece of guidance and explain how you made it accurate and accessible
- Describe how you would evaluate whether people learned or changed a work practice, including the limits of your measures
- Explain how you would protect learner privacy and make reporting a mistake feel safe and useful
- Identify a security or process change that should accompany training when training alone cannot address the risk

## Certifications

There is no widely used certification ladder for this path. Build security fundamentals and demonstrate instructional design, communication and evaluation skills. Employers may value teaching or learning-design experience; check the requirements for the specific role. See [Certifications](../guides/certifications.md).
